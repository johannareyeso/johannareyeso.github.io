title: "Women After Revolution"
collection: publications
status: "Working Papers"
abstract: "Why do some revolutions advance women’s empowerment while others do not? Scholars agree that conflict can open windows of opportunity for gender equality, yet how different types of revolution shape these outcomes remains unexplored. I argue that revolutions durably empower women when they fuse women's emancipation with national sovereignty \textit{and} institutionalize mass women's organizations as state-building tools. The first makes rolling back women's gains a betrayal of the new nation; the second gives women a standing channel to press their demands from below. Triangulating across six months of fieldwork, original archival, survey, and administrative data, and over 90 interviews with revolutionaries in Cuba, I document how women's organizations leveraged an inclusive national liberation ideology to extract ministerial concessions, shape family law, and normalize women's leadership at the local level. I then set Cuba against a global panel of 345 revolutionary episodes since 1900, using a staggered-adoption difference-in-differences design. National-liberation social revolutions produce the largest gains in women's empowerment---an average treatment effect more than five times that of revolutions that were neither anticolonial nor social. The findings underscore how broad-based mobilization from below can generate gains for marginalized groups, even within authoritarian regimes. Contact for working paper."
